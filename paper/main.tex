\begin{abstract}
Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) offer human-interpretable reasoning by predicting intermediate concepts before the final classification. However, state-of-the-art architectures still rely on flat concept spaces, neglecting the inherent taxonomical structure of real-world objects. This paper introduces the Hierarchical Coarse-to-Fine Label-Free Concept Bottleneck Model (Hier-CBM). Our architecture strictly enforces a cognitive hierarchy at both the class and the concept level, implementing a dynamic hard-gating mechanism that completely prunes logically incompatible fine-grained concepts based on coarse superclass predictions. Evaluated on a high-resolution version of CIFAR-100 maintaining a rigorous two-tier taxonomy, our approach reduces active concepts to just 5.4 under oracle conditions, achieves zero catastrophic errors upon human intervention, and presents an interpretable sparsity-accuracy trade-off compared to black-box baselines.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Concept Bottleneck Models, Explainable AI, Hierarchical Learning, Label-Free CBM, Hard-Gating}

\section{Introduction}
The rapid proliferation of deep learning has been met with a growing demand for interpretability. Concept-based Explainable Artificial Intelligence (C-XAI) addresses this by anchoring the decision-making process to human-understandable visual attributes~\cite{Poeta_2025}. A canonical approach is the Concept Bottleneck Model (CBM), which factorizes classification into a concept prediction phase followed by a label prediction phase. 

Traditionally, acquiring concept annotations was a prohibitive bottleneck. Recent advancements introduced unsupervised and label-free discovery paradigms~\cite{Wang_2023}. Notably, the Label-Free CBM (LF-CBM) leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) and vision-language architectures like CLIP to automatically generate and ground visual concepts without manual labels~\cite{Xie_2026, Pastor_2026_tutorial}.

Despite removing the supervision bottleneck, current LF-CBMs overwhelmingly treat concepts as a "flat" array, pooling all visual attributes at a single semantic granularity. Human reasoning, however, is inherently stratified: we first categorize an object at the superordinate level (e.g., ``vehicle''), and only then discriminate at the subordinate level (e.g., ``train'' vs. ``truck''). In this project, we explicitly encode this cognitive behavior into the network's topology. Our proposed architecture enforces hierarchy \textbf{simultaneously at two levels}: at the \textit{class level}, by structuring predictions across 20 superclasses and 100 fine-grained classes; and at the \textit{concept level}, by constructing two entirely separate concept dictionaries---one for coarse macro-category discrimination, one for fine-grained micro-class discrimination. A dynamic Coarse-to-Fine Hard Gating mechanism then rigidly couples these two hierarchies, masking conceptually incompatible fine-grained features during inference.

\section{Related work}
Early works demonstrated the efficacy of hierarchical reasoning to allow models to fail more gracefully~\cite{Hase_2019}. As CBMs matured, \textbf{Lin et al.}~\cite{Lin_2022} proposed Progressive CBMs for fetal ultrasound assessment, building a two-stage pipeline where visual segmentations (organs) gate the prediction of higher-level properties. While pioneering in its sequential structure, this approach is domain-specific and does not decouple concept and label hierarchies. \textbf{Panousis et al.}~\cite{Panousis_2023} introduced Coarse-to-Fine CBMs (CF-CBMs), using data-driven Bernoulli variables to mask irrelevant patch-level attributes based on high-level concepts. However, CF-CBMs address the \textit{concept} space only and ultimately predict a \textbf{flat label}, leaving the class hierarchy unexploited.

To directly combat ``concept leakage''---a phenomenon where the final classifier bypasses concepts and latches onto raw spurious features---\textbf{Sun et al.}~\cite{Sun_2024} proposed SUPCBM, which enforces concept supervision with a hard binary intervention matrix. Their follow-up work, Hi-CBM~\cite{Sun_2026}, extends this into a two-level concept hierarchy (perceptual and descriptive) using Concept-Bottleneck Pooling to aggressively filter spurious signals. Crucially, both SUPCBM and Hi-CBM apply hierarchy exclusively in the \textit{concept space}, while the mapping from concepts to classes remains fully learned and unconstrained, leaving the gate between coarse and fine semantics implicit. \textbf{Xie et al.}~\cite{Xie_2026} proposed HIL-CBM, which operates in the label-free setting and uses a Tree-path KL Divergence Loss to enforce semantic consistency across abstraction levels. While HIL-CBM is the closest in spirit to our work, it relies on \textit{soft} probabilistic losses during training to guide hierarchy, rather than enforcing a \textit{hard structural constraint} at inference time. \textbf{Uyterlinde et al.}~\cite{Uyterlinde_2026} took a radically geometric approach with HypCBM, embedding concepts in hyperbolic space to natively capture entailment relationships and propagate interventions hierarchically. Although elegant, this formulation changes the optimization geometry entirely, adding significant complexity and lacking the transparent, discrete gating semantics our architecture provides. Finally, \textbf{Bertinetto et al.}~\cite{Bertinetto_2020} pioneered hierarchy-aware evaluation through Hierarchical Cross-Entropy, establishing that errors should be penalized proportionally to their semantic severity.

\section{Research gaps}
Based on our literature review, we identify the following critical gaps in current C-XAI research:

\textbf{1. Flat Concept Representations Persist in Practice:} Despite the documented advantages of hierarchical reasoning~\cite{Hase_2019, Uyterlinde_2026}, the vast majority of deployed CBMs---including label-free variants~\cite{Xie_2026, Pastor_2026_tutorial}---still ground classification in a single, monolithic concept pool. This conflates macro-level discriminative features (e.g., \textit{has wings, has wheels}) with micro-level ones (e.g., \textit{has a jet engine, has pedals}), making it impossible to isolate which level of abstraction drives a prediction. The result is a bottleneck that is interpretable in name but semantically unstructured in practice.

\textbf{2. Concept Hierarchy and Class Hierarchy Are Decoupled:} The most relevant prior works~\cite{Sun_2026, Panousis_2023} enforce hierarchy in the \textit{concept space} independently from the \textit{label space}. Hi-CBM~\cite{Sun_2026} organizes concepts into perceptual and descriptive tiers, but the final label predictor remains a fully unconstrained black-box that can freely mix signals from both tiers. CF-CBM~\cite{Panousis_2023} hierarchically masks patch concepts but outputs a flat classification. HIL-CBM~\cite{Xie_2026} introduces multi-level predictions but relies on soft regularization losses rather than structural constraints. The field therefore lacks a framework that \textit{rigidly couples} concept granularity with class granularity through a hard, discrete mechanism.

\textbf{3. Hierarchy-Aware Evaluation Is Not Integrated into Concept-Level Analysis:} Bertinetto et al.~\cite{Bertinetto_2020} established that errors should be weighted by semantic severity. Yet modern hierarchical CBMs rarely close the loop between their structural design and their evaluation protocol. In particular, no existing work formally asks whether enforcing a dual hierarchy---at both the class and the concept level---specifically causes the model to make semantically ``better'' mistakes, i.e., errors confined within the correct superclass.

To address these three interconnected gaps, our work introduces a framework that enforces a \textbf{simultaneous dual-hierarchy} in both the class space and the concept space, using a deterministic hard-gating constraint, and evaluates its behavior with hierarchy-aware Lowest Common Ancestor (LCA) metrics.

\section{Methodology}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, height=\textheight, keepaspectratio]{figures/architecture_ver2.png}
    \caption{Overview of the Hierarchical Coarse-to-Fine Hard Gating Architecture. \\ \small The framework explicitly separates coarse and fine concepts. A superclass prediction acts as a hard mask, dynamically pruning logically incompatible fine-grained concepts before final classification.}
    \label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

Our architecture (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}) introduces a \textbf{Coarse-to-Fine Hard Gating} mechanism that intrinsically enforces taxonomy at two levels simultaneously. We explicitly constructed a custom High-Resolution CIFAR-100 dataset to retain the rigorous two-tier hierarchy (20 Superclasses, 100 Fine Classes) without suffering the degradation of upsampling native 32x32 images, which is necessary to extract high-quality CLIP embeddings.

\textbf{Relation to Standard CBMs.} Traditional CBMs consist of a \textit{concept encoder}---a full neural network trained to predict concept scores from images, requiring explicit per-concept annotations---followed by a \textit{label predictor}. Training can be sequential, independent, or joint~\cite{Koh_2020}. Our framework offers a more scalable alternative to this paradigm. Following the Label-Free CBM approach~\cite{Pastor_2026_tutorial}, \textbf{no concept encoder is trained}. Instead, the backbone (CLIP ViT-B/32) is kept \textit{frozen}: concept scores are computed as dot-product similarities between the frozen CLIP image embeddings and the frozen CLIP text embeddings of the LLM-generated concept phrases, requiring zero concept-level supervision. The only learned components are two \textit{sparse linear classifiers}---one coarse, one fine---trained on these pre-computed concept scores via L1-regularized logistic regression (SAGA solver, $C{=}0.5$). During training, the fine classifier is fitted using \textit{teacher forcing}: the binary concept mask is applied using the ground-truth superclass label, rather than the predicted one, providing a clean upper-bound signal. At inference, the mask is driven by the coarse classifier's own prediction, making the gating fully autonomous.

The methodology unfolds across two primary phases, precisely mirroring the dual-hierarchy design illustrated in our architecture:

\textbf{Offline Phase: Label-Free Concept Generation.} Following the LF-CBM paradigm~\cite{Pastor_2026_tutorial}, we ran an offline concept generation pipeline using a locally-hosted \textbf{Gemma-4-12B-IT} model (served via LM Studio). To enforce output quality, the model was configured with a \textit{visual-only} system prompt that constrained generation to physical, observable features (shapes, colors, textures, body parts) and explicitly excluded behaviors, habitats, or any invisible trait. Low temperature ($T{=}0.2$) and structured JSON array output enforcement ensured deterministic, parseable outputs with a repeat penalty to reduce redundant phrasing.

Two entirely \textit{separate} generation passes were executed, creating a concept-level hierarchy by construction:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Coarse Concept Bank} ($\mathcal{C}_{coarse}$): For each of the 20 CIFAR-100 superclasses, the model was prompted to list the most important \textit{macro-level visual features} for identifying objects in that broad category (e.g., \textit{``has fur'', ``has wheels'', ``is metallic''}). Each superclass received 15 candidate concepts.
    \item \textit{Fine Concept Bank} ($\mathcal{C}_{fine}$): For each of the 100 fine-grained classes, the model was prompted using a \textit{sibling-aware} strategy: the prompt explicitly listed all sibling classes within the same superclass and instructed the model to generate features that \textit{differentiate} the target class from its siblings (e.g., for \textit{``motorcycle''}, features like \textit{``exposed engine block''} are preferred over generic features like \textit{``has two wheels''}). Each fine class received 15 candidate concepts.
\end{itemize}

Raw outputs were then passed through a two-stage \textbf{concept filtering pipeline}. First, a \textit{length and synonym filter} removed concepts exceeding 45 characters and flagged those with cosine similarity $> 0.85$ to their own class name (measured with the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} SentenceTransformer), preventing trivially tautological features. Second, an \textit{activation-based functional filter} computed raw CLIP (ViT-B/32) cosine similarities between concept text embeddings and image embeddings from the training set, retaining only the top-$k$ most visually grounded concepts per class.

The resulting filtered concept banks were then encoded into static CLIP text embeddings. During training, image-concept similarity scores were computed as cosine similarities between CLIP image features and these static embeddings. A linear \textit{concept projection layer} was trained with a cosine-cubed alignment loss, and a final sparse linear classifier was fit using the GLM-SAGA elastic-net optimizer~\cite{Pastor_2026_tutorial} to encourage a small active concept footprint.

\textbf{Runtime Phase: Inference and Hard Gating.} At runtime, the image is passed through the vision encoder, and classification proceeds in a two-tier sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Coarse Bottleneck:} A coarse logistic regression evaluates the image using \textit{exclusively} the Coarse Concept Bank $\mathcal{C}_{coarse}$, predicting one of the 20 superclasses. This stage is explicitly blind to all fine-grained concepts.
    \item \textit{Hard Gating \& Fine Prediction:} The predicted superclass acts as a deterministic hard mask. It sets the CLIP-grounded activations of all logically incompatible Fine Concepts to exactly zero (e.g., if ``Vehicles'' is predicted, the concept \textit{``has fur''} is completely deactivated). The final fine-grained classification relies exclusively on the surviving, semantically coherent concepts. This enforces the \textbf{class-level hierarchy}: the prediction space for the fine stage is structurally constrained by the coarse decision.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Experiments and analysis}
To validate our approach, we first analyze the quality of the generated concepts and then benchmark our Hierarchical CBM (Hier. CBM) against a standard Flat LF-CBM and a black-box CLIP Linear Probe. 

\subsection{Validation of Generated Concepts}
Since our approach relies entirely on Label-Free concept generation, it is crucial that the LLM produces semantically cohesive and distinct concepts. Figure~\ref{fig:per_sc} shows a t-SNE projection of the CLIP text embeddings for the generated coarse concepts. The distinct clustering confirms that CLIP's latent space natively understands the taxonomical separation of the 20 superclasses, independent of our trained model.

Furthermore, to validate that these textual concepts align with visual inputs, Figure~\ref{fig:oracle} displays the raw cosine similarity (activations) between images from the 'Vehicles 1' superclass and the concept bank. \subsection{Hierarchy-Aware Mistake Evaluation}
Standard Top-1 Accuracy fails to capture the semantic severity of errors. We utilized the Lowest Common Ancestor (LCA) Error Distance, where an error within the same superclass (sibling mistake) has $LCA=1$, and an error across different superclasses (catastrophic mistake) has $LCA=2$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_lca_distribution.png}
    \caption{Hierarchy-Aware Error Distribution (LCA). \\ \small Comparison of error types across models. The Hierarchical CBM (Ours) favors sibling mistakes over catastrophic errors compared to flat baselines.}
    \label{fig:lca}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:lca}, our Hier. CBM exhibits a higher catastrophic error rate (4.6\% LCA=2) compared to the Flat LF-CBM (3.0\% LCA=2). This is a direct consequence of the hard-gating design: when the coarse classifier misroutes an image to the wrong superclass, the subsequent mask blocks the correct fine concepts entirely, producing a cross-superclass error that a flat model would not incur. However, this cost is the flip side of a structural guarantee: as demonstrated by the Oracle condition (0.0\% LCA=2), a single, coarse-level human intervention is sufficient to completely eliminate all catastrophic errors, reducing the Avg LCA Error Distance from 0.167 to 0.081. This underscores the key interpretability advantage of our architecture: the hierarchy makes the source of catastrophic failures transparent and actionable.

Table~\ref{tab:results} reveals the profound impact of our hard-gating architecture under intervention. The key takeaway is that a single high-level Oracle correction on the superclass cascades down through the hard gate, boosting the model's overall accuracy to 91.91\%. This allows our interpretable Hierarchical CBM to close the performance gap and achieve an accuracy that is highly competitive with the black-box CLIP probe (96.42\%) and the standard Flat LF-CBM (93.50\%), while maintaining a strict semantic taxonomy that naturally precludes any cross-superclass ($LCA=2$) errors.

More importantly, the hard gate drastically reduces the cognitive load required to understand the model's final decision. While the model initially uses an average of 46 concepts to find the superclass, once the superclass is fixed (either by the network or the Oracle), it requires an average of only \textbf{5.4 active fine concepts} to determine the final class. \section{Conclusions}
In this project, we proposed a Hierarchical Coarse-to-Fine Label-Free Concept Bottleneck Model to address the limitations of flat CBM representations and evaluation strategies. By embedding a rigorous dual-level hierarchy spanning both class labels and conceptual features, and introducing a dynamic hard-gating mechanism, we effectively bridged the gap between structural concept definitions and model inference. Our evaluation using hierarchy-aware LCA metrics demonstrates that our architecture fails more gracefully than flat alternatives and is highly receptive to human intervention, reducing cognitive load to just over 5 active concepts per decision.
